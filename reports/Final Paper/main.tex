\begin{abstract}
    This final project explores the potential benefits of incorporating graph geometry into stock forecasting models, specifically focusing on the S\&P 100 index. We implement two baseline models, a Random Forest and an LSTM, and compare their performance against a Graph Neural Network (GNN) that captures the relationships between stocks using a graph structure. Beyond evaluating the forecasting performance of the models, we aim to analyze whether graph symmetries might or not be the optimal inductive bias to include in stock forecasting models. Forcing a model to be invariant to certain symmetries may be beneficial in some cases, but it may also limit the model's ability to capture important relationships in the data and, as we hypothesize, incetivize the model to learn spurious relationships that aren't actually present in the data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Stock forecasting is a challenging task that, due to its economic implications, has been an attractive area of research for decades. In specific, AI and machine learning techniques have been widely applied to this problem, with varying degrees of success. Despite the advances in the matter, many current approaches are limited to using technical analysis to capture historical trends of each stock price. In this paper, we explore the potential benefits of incorporating graph geometry into stock forecasting models, specifically focusing on the S\&P 100 index. We decide to focus on this subset of the stock market to reduce the complexity of the problem and to be able to focus on the relationships between the stocks, which is where we expect graph geometry to provide the most value.

In indeces such as the S\&P 100, there are important relationships between individual stocks, such as shared market sectors or financial indicators, that may not be fully captured by traditional forecasting models and that could be better modeled using graph-based approaches. By representing the stocks as nodes in a graph and their relationships as edges, we can leverage geometric deep learning techniques to capture these interactions and, potentially, improve forecasting performance

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of graph geometry in stock forecasting, we first implement two baseline models: a Random Forest and an LSTM. These models will serve as benchmarks to compare against a Graph Neural Network (GNN). The choice of these architectures is motivated by their widespread use in time series forecasting and their ability to capture different aspects of the data. The Random Forest is a powerful ensemble method that can capture non-linear relationships (and, given a sufficiently informative feature set, obtain performance comparable to more complex models), while the LSTM is a type of recurrent neural network that is particularly well-suited for sequential data (in this case, the temporal evolution of the index). The GNN, on the other hand, is designed to capture the relationships between stocks by modeling them as a graph, which is where we expect to see the most significant improvements in forecasting performance.

We model the relationships between stocks using a graph structure, where nodes represent individual stocks and edges represent relationships, mainly shared market sectors, correlations between stock returns, and divergences in the returns' distributions. The problem is thus framed as a node classification task, in which we aim to predict the direction of the stock price movement (up, neutral, or down) for each stock in the index within a given time frame. We evaluate the performance of the models using standard metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, and we analyze the results to understand the impact of incorporating graph geometry into the forecasting process.

\section{Related Work}

Financial forecasting surged as a research area in the late 19th century, with the introduction of statistical methods (such as those developed by Irving Fisher) to analyze market trends. It wasn't, though, until the mid-20th century that advances in computing power allowed for the application of more complex models. All in all, we can roughly divide the evolution of financial forecasting into three main eras: the pre-computing era, the statistical era, and the machine learning era. The pre-computing era was characterized by the use of simple heuristics and technical analysis to predict stock prices.

The statistical era saw the introduction of parametric models that, while much more expressive than the previous heuristics, were based upon explicit assumptions about the underlying data distribution \cite{traditional_dl_stock_prediction}. For example, ARIMA models the conditional mean of the time series as a linear function of past observations and errors, while GARCH models the conditional variance as a function of past squared errors and variances.

The machine learning era, currently under process, is characterized by the use of non-parametric models that can capture complex relationships in the data without making strong assumptions about the underlying distribution. The last decades have seen a surge in implementations of machine learning models for financial forecasting, including general-purpose models such as Random Forests \cite{rf_stock_prediction} and time-series specific models such as LSTMs \cite{stock_prediction_lstm} and Gateted Recurrent Units (GRUs) \cite{rnn_gru_lstm_comparison}. With the introduction of the Transformer architecture \cite{attention_is_all_you_need}, attention-based architectures surged as a promising alternative to recurrent models, as they allow for long-range dependencies to be captured more effectively and in a more parallelizable way. In the specific context of stock forecasting, attention-based models have been shown to outperform recurrent models in certain settings \cite{attention_transformers_stock_prediction}.

Last, there has been a growing interest in the use of graph-based models for financial forecasting. Lead by the assumption that the relationships between stocks can provide valuable information for forecasting (and may not be fully captured by traditional models, as they lack the necessary inductive bias to model these relationships), the last years have witnessed a boom in the implementation of GNN-based models for stock forecasting. These range from purely graph-based models \cite{gnn_stock_prediction} to hybrid models that combine graph-based and sequence-based architectures \cite{survey_temporal_gnn,lstm_plus_gnn_stock_prediction}. Added to this, the hybrid paradigm has evolved from simple combinations of sequence-based and graph-based models to more complex architectures in which the graph structure is integrated into the sequence-based model itself, such as TCGNs (Temporal Convolutional Graph Networks) \cite{temporal_gnn_stock_prediction}.

In general, the results in the literature suggest that incorporating graph geometry into stock forecasting models can lead to improved performance, especially when the relationships between stocks are strong and informative. This does come with a caveat, though, as the performance of graph-based models can be sensitive to the choice of graph structure and the quality of the data used to construct the graph (more so than with simpler models as LSTMs or Random Forests). In this sense, we hypothesize that, due to the variety in graphs we may construct on a given index (for example, based on different financial indicators or with different thresholds for edge creation), the performance of the GNN may vary significantly depending on the graph structure used. Thus, the results of this project will additionally contribute to the understanding of the impact of graph structure on forecasting performance, which is an important aspect to consider when applying graph-based models to financial forecasting.

\section{Background}

Throughout the project, we will be using the following models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest}: an ensemble learning method that constructs a multitude of decision trees during training and outputs the majority class as the prediction. Given a sufficiently informative feature set, Random Forests can capture complex relationships in the data and achieve competitive performance even compared to more complex models.
    \item \textbf{LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)}: a type of recurrent neural network that is particularly well-suited for sequential data (and handles long-range dependencies better than its predecessors, such as vanilla RNNs).
    \item \textbf{GNN (Graph Neural Network)}: a type of neural network designed to operate on graph-structured data. In our case, we will be using a specific architecture that combines an LSTM to capture the temporal evolution of each stock with a GAT (Graph Attention Network)
\end{itemize}

Focusing on the mathematics behind the GNN, we can describe GAT as a particular case of the more general message-passing framework for GNNs. In this framework, the representation of each node is updated by aggregating information from its neighbors. Specifically, in a GAT layer, the representation of a node $i$ at layer $l+1$ is computed as follows:
\begin{equation}
    h_u^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left(\sum_{v \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \alpha_{uv}^{(l)} W^{(l)} h_v^{(l)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $h_u^{(l)}$ is the representation of node $u$ at layer $l$, $\mathcal{N}(u)$ is the set of neighbors of node $u$, $W^{(l)}$ is a learnable weight matrix, $\sigma$ is a non-linear activation function (ELU in our case), and $\alpha_{uv}^{(l)}$ is the attention coefficient that determines the importance of node $v$'s features to node $u$ at layer $l$ (learned during training). Thus, it can also be interpreted as a generalization of the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) layer, with the information from the neighbors being weighted by the attention coefficients instead of being averaged.

These weights are computed, by means of a Neural Network, as:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{uv}^{(l)} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(a^T [W^{(l)} h_u^{(l)} || W^{(l)} h_v^{(l)}]\right)\right)}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(a^T [W^{(l)} h_u^{(l)} || W^{(l)} h_k^{(l)}]\right)\right)}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is a learnable weight vector and $||$ denotes concatenation. This allows the model to learn different weights for different neighbors, which can be particularly useful in our case, as some relationships between stocks may be more informative than others for forecasting.

The computation of the hidden state \(h_u^{(l+1)}\) for each node \(u\) in the GAT layer is performed in parallel for each head (so that different heads may learn different aspects of the relationships between stocks) and then concatenated to form the final representation for the next layer.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The data used in this project consists of the historical stock prices of the companies included in the S\&P 100 index, along with relevant financial indicators, depending on the specific model. We use the following variables, common for all models, as features for each stock:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identifiers and Basic Information:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Ticker symbol
              \item Date
              \item Adjusted closing price
              \item Daily Log Return: $\log\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)$, where $P_t$ is the adjusted closing price at time $t$.
              \item Future Weekly Log Return: $\sum_{i=1}^{5}\log\left(\frac{P_{t+i}}{P_{t+i-1}}\right)$, used only to define the prediction target 5 trading days ahead.
                    % Explicar más de cómo obtenemos la label en la siguiente sección
              \item Class label: a categorical variable indicating the direction of the stock price movement (up, neutral, down), defined in terms of the sign of the future Weekly Log Return.
          \end{itemize}
    \item Volume:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Daily trading volume
              \item Normalized trading volume: the daily trading volume divided by the 20-day rolling average trading volume of the same stock.
          \end{itemize}
    \item Technical Indicators:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Weekly Moving Average (5MA): the average of the adjusted closing price over the past 5 trading days.
              \item Monthly Moving Average (20MA): the average of the adjusted closing price over the past 20 trading days.
              \item Relative Strength Index (RSI): a momentum oscillator that measures the speed and change of price movements, calculated as $RSI = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + RS}$, where $RS$ is the average gain of up periods divided by the average loss of down periods over a specified time frame (14 trading days in our case).
              \item Rolling Volatility: the standard deviation of the daily log returns over a rolling window of 20 trading days.
          \end{itemize}
    \item Structural Information:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Market sector: a categorical variable indicating the market sector to which the stock belongs (e.g., Technology, Healthcare, Financials, etc.).
              \item Market Cap: a continuous variable indicating the market capitalization of the company (in dollars).
          \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

In addition to this, for the LSTM model, we include the sequence of the past 20 days of the above features as input, while for the GNN model, we construct two different graph structures based on the following relationships between stocks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A correlation graph, where each stock is connected to its top-$k$ most correlated neighbors using training-set stock returns, with additional sector-similarity edges.
    \item A distributional-similarity graph, where each stock is connected to its top-$k$ closest neighbors according to the Jensen-Shannon divergence between return distributions, with additional sector-similarity edges.
\end{itemize}

All data was obtained from Yahoo Finance using the \texttt{yfinance} Python library, for the period between January 4, 2021 and December 31, 2025. The choice of this period is motivated by two main reasons: (i) 5 years is a common time frame used in the literature for stock forecasting, as it provides a good balance between the amount of data available for training and the relevance of the data (as older data may not be as relevant for forecasting future stock movements), and (ii) it avoids, at least theoretically, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the stock market, which introduced a significant amount of noise and structural changes in the market that could have affected the performance of the models \footnote{After careful analysis, we assumed that the turmoil caused by the COVID-19 pandemic in the stock market had a significant impact until the end of 2020, and that, from January 2021 onwards, the market had stabilized enough for the data to be considered as not significantly affected by the pandemic.}.

\subsection{Data and Preprocessing}

In order to obtain the information that we are to feed into our models, we followed the following steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We obtained the list of stocks in the S\&P 100 index, along with their corresponding ticker symbols. In specific, we filtered this list to the stocks that have been in the index for the entire period of time considered (January 4, 2021 to December 31, 2025), which resulted in a total of 88 stocks. Even though this filtering step reduces the number of stocks we are considering, we consider the reduction small enough to not significantly affect the performance of the models, while it allows us to avoid dealing with structural changes in the index.
    \item For each stock, we obtained the data mentioned in the previous section, computing the necessary features. As to the labels, we decided to define a threshold $\epsilon$ on the future Weekly Log Return to determine the class labels, in order to avoid labeling small fluctuations as significant movements. Specifically, we defined the class labels as follows:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Up: if the future Weekly Log Return is greater than $\epsilon$
              \item Neutral: if the future Weekly Log Return is between $-\epsilon$ and $\epsilon$
              \item Down: if the future Weekly Log Return is less than $-\epsilon$
          \end{itemize}
          We set $\epsilon$ to 0.01, which corresponds to a 1\% change in the stock price over a week. Though this threshold is rather arbitrary, it yields a good balance between the number of samples in each class, as mentioned in \ref{appendix:class_distribution}.
    \item We split the data into training, validation, and test sets, using a chronological split to avoid data leakage. Specifically, training data ends on December 31, 2023, validation data ends on December 31, 2024, and the remaining observations are used for testing. This divides the dataset into roughly 3, 1, and 1 years, respectively, and makes the splits comparable across models.

          This is done in different ways for the different models, though. For the Random Forest, we assign the split in the tabular dataset according to the date of each sample. For the LSTM, we create sequences of 20 days for each stock and assign their split according to the reference date of each sequence. For the GNN, we create the graph structure using the data from the training set, and then split the snapshots of the graph (this is, every node and edge at a given time step).
\end{itemize}

These preprocessing steps are implemented as a reproducible pipeline that can be executed from the repository root with \texttt{uv run python -m src.data.run\_pipeline}.


\subsection{Models and Hyperparameters}

The GNN experiments are trained using AdamW with weight decay, cross-entropy loss, gradient clipping, a fixed random seed, and early stopping based on validation macro-F1. The best checkpoint according to validation macro-F1 is saved during training and reloaded before computing the final test score.

The architectures of our models are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest}: we use a Random Forest with 200 trees, a maximum depth of 12, and a minimum of 50 samples per split and 20 per leaf. We add class-weighting to handle the slight class imbalance in the dataset.
    \item \textbf{LSTM}: we use a 3-layer bidirectional LSTM with a hidden size of 128, followed by two-layer MLP with hidden sizes of 64 and 32, respectively, ReLU activations and dropout of 0.2. We train it with AdamW with a learning rate of 5e-4, weight decay of 1e-3, class-weighted cross-entropy loss, and early stopping with a patience of 15 epochs.
    \item \textbf{GNN}: Our GNN architecture combines a temporal encoder with a graph attention network. Each node corresponds to one stock and carries a 20-day feature window. We first encode this sequence with a 2-layer bidirectional LSTM (hidden size 128) and apply a learned temporal attention mechanism to aggregate the per-day information into a single node embedding. We include a visualization of the architecture in Figure \ref{fig:gat_architecture}.

          On top of these embeddings, we stack two \texttt{GATConv} layers with edge features: the first has 4 attention heads and a hidden size of 64 (per head), the second aggregates them into a single 64-dimensional representation. We include Batch Normalization (to deal with the inherent non-stationarity of the data), ELU activations, residual connections (to avoid over-smoothing and vanishing gradients), and dropout of 0.3.

          Finally, we apply a 2-layer MLP with hidden sizes of 64 and 32, respectively, ReLU activations and dropout of 0.3, to obtain the final class probabilities. We train the model with AdamW with a learning rate of 6e-4, weight decay of 1e-4, class-weighted cross-entropy loss, gradient clipping with a max norm of 1.0, and early stopping with a patience of 40 epochs (to allow the model to converge given the complexity of the architecture and the noisiness of the data).
\end{itemize}

    \label{appendix:class_distribution}

    The distribution of the class labels (up, neutral, down), given $\epsilon = 0.01$, is as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Up: 41.3\%
        \item Neutral: 24.85\%
        \item Down: 33.85\%
    \end{itemize}

    This distribution is relatively balanced and, moreover, may reflect a certain intuition about the stock market, in which, over a week, stocks are more likely to go up or down than to remain neutral (as small fluctuations are not labeled as significant movements) and, overall, there is a slightly higher probability of stocks going up than down (which may be due to the general growth trend of the stock market over time). This distribution should allow the models to learn effectively without being biased towards a particular class.
